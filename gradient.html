Rare positions, strong signal

Every outcome private messaging predicts, ordered by how many people hold it

— outcomes are stacked by how common the position is, rarest at the top. Each row is one odds ratio for people who got political news that way in the last twenty-four hours against people who did not, with party, education, age, income, gender, survey date and every other news source held equal. The vertical ordering comes from outside the estimates, so nothing about the plot forces the dots into a line.

For private messaging they form one anyway. The association is positive and largest where almost nobody holds the position, and reverses where most people do — from — on the rarest to — on the most common.

That is only worth saying if it belongs to this source rather than to the odds scale. Switch the source and the ordering stays put while the dots rearrange: — of the — slope the other way. The two panels on the right run that comparison across every source at once, and run the obvious mechanical alternative against it.

The same points on a real axis

A forest plot's rows are evenly spaced, so the staircase on the left is ordinal — it cannot carry a fitted line. Here prevalence is the horizontal position, which is the axis a slope is entitled to.

Does every source do this?

If the gradient were an artifact of the odds scale it would appear for all seven. Slope is the correlation between prevalence and the log odds ratio: negative means the source predicts rare positions. Rare ÷ common divides the average odds ratio below 25% by the average at 40% and above.

Is it just the arithmetic?

An odds ratio has more room to move when a split is lopsided, so a source could look like this without predicting anything in particular. That alternative predicts large odds ratios at both ends, not positive ones at the rare end. The test separates them: correlate the size of the association, ignoring its sign, with distance from an even split.

Reading it correctly

Orientation is an interpretive choice, and it is the page's, not the model's. Eleven of these outcomes are trust questions and six are belief questions, and they only share an axis once trust is turned around to face the same way as belief — so "the adverse side" means believing the claim, or distrusting the institution. Turning an outcome around inverts its odds ratio and replaces its prevalence with 100 minus itself; nothing else changes. The As the question was asked button undoes it, and the gradient is worth checking there.

These are cross-sectional associations, confounded with selective exposure by construction. They fix the ordering of predictors, not transmission rates. A person who already holds a rare position is freer to choose a messaging app than the app is to give them the position, and this design cannot tell those apart.

Toggling a source changes which fitted model is displayed, not what was fitted. All seven sources were in every model together. Subgroups are separately fitted models, and the variable defining a subgroup leaves the control set inside it.

Thin rows are marked †. Outcomes differ in how many waves carry them — some run in all eleven, some in four, and the childhood MMR item in one, which is why that row alone carries no wave fixed effect. The dagger marks any outcome fielded in fewer than half the waves of the best-covered one, so it tracks wave coverage rather than sample size: inside a party subgroup every row is smaller, but the coverage gaps between outcomes are the same ones. Hovering a row gives both numbers.
