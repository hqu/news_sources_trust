Rare positions, strong signal

Every outcome private messaging predicts, ordered by how many people hold it

— outcomes are stacked by how common the position is, rarest at the top. Every question is shown the way it was asked: the percentage beside a row is the share who gave that answer, and the odds ratio is for giving it. Each row compares people who got political news that way in the last twenty-four hours with people who did not, with party, education, age, income, gender, survey date and every other news source held equal. The vertical ordering comes from outside the estimates, so nothing about the plot forces the dots into a line.

The same points on a real axis

A forest plot's rows are evenly spaced, so the staircase on the left is ordinal — it cannot carry a fitted line. Here prevalence is the horizontal position, which is the axis a slope is entitled to.

Does every source do this?

If the gradient were an artifact of the odds scale it would appear for all seven. Slope is the correlation between prevalence and the log odds ratio: negative means the source predicts rare positions. Rare ÷ common divides the average odds ratio below 25% by the average at 40% and above.

Is it just the arithmetic?

The worry is that a source could show a gradient without predicting anything in particular. It is worth being exact about which alternative that is. An odds ratio is already the scale that stays put when prevalence changes and the underlying effect does not, so the logistic scale is not the culprit. The real alternative is an effect that is constant in percentage points: add six points to everyone and the odds ratio is enormous at 4% and near 1.0 at 50%, with no gradient in the underlying effect at all.

That story cannot change sign. A constant percentage-point effect leaves every odds ratio on the same side of 1.0. Crossing below is where the fitted line passes 1.0, and the last two columns count outcomes whose interval clears 1.0 on each side. A source with a solid count in both columns has changed sign, and no constant-effect-on-any-scale story survives that.

Reading it correctly

By default nothing is reoriented. Eleven of these outcomes are trust questions and six are belief questions, and each is shown as it was fielded: the row says Trust in Donald Trump, the percentage is the 39.0% who do, and the odds ratio is for trusting him. The cost is that the horizontal axis means "more likely to give this answer" rather than one substantive thing, since the affirmative answer is agreement on a belief item and trust on a trust item.

Turned to the adverse side buys a single-meaning axis at the price of an interpretive choice that is the page's and not the model's. It inverts the odds ratio, replaces the prevalence with 100 minus itself, and renames the row, so Trust in Donald Trump at 39.0% becomes Distrust of Donald Trump at 61.0% and every outcome faces the adverse way — believing the claim, or distrusting the institution. That is the convention explorer.html uses. It is worth pressing: the gradient is steeper there, and how much steeper is a fact about the choice rather than about private messaging.

These are cross-sectional associations, confounded with selective exposure by construction. They fix the ordering of predictors, not transmission rates. A person who already holds a rare position is freer to choose a messaging app than the app is to give them the position, and this design cannot tell those apart.

Toggling a source changes which fitted model is displayed, not what was fitted. All seven sources were in every model together. Subgroups are separately fitted models, and the variable defining a subgroup leaves the control set inside it.

Thin rows are marked †. Outcomes differ in how many waves carry them — some run in all eleven and some in four. The dagger marks any outcome fielded in fewer than half the waves of the best-covered one, so it tracks wave coverage rather than sample size: inside a party subgroup every row is smaller, but the coverage gaps between outcomes are the same ones. Hovering a row gives both numbers. One outcome in the payload is not drawn at all — approval of the childhood MMR mandate ran in a single wave, so no wave fixed effect could be fitted for it and it is not comparable with the rest. It is hidden here and on the other three data pages rather than deleted from the payload.
