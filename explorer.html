Rare beliefs and common beliefs go with different news sources

Some false claims are believed by about one American in seven. Others are believed by two in three. It turns out that the news sources linked to the rare claims are not the ones linked to the common claims — and podcasts sit apart from everything else. Below: 18 survey questions and 8 kinds of news source from the CHIP50 panel, from weighted models that hold party, education, age, income, gender and survey date constant. Everything is shown so that to the right of 1 is the worse direction — believing the claim, or distrusting the institution. These are associations, not causes: people choose their news as well as being shaped by it.

Start here

Eighteen outcomes, eight ways of getting political news.

Each dot is one regime's association with one outcome. Its position across is the odds ratio; to the right of the heavy line means the regime predicts the adverse direction — believing a false or conspiratorial claim, or distrusting an institution.

How far up a dot sits is how much of the population is already in that adverse state. Reading the chart bottom to top is watching a belief spread, and that axis turns out to matter more than the topic does.

Takeaway 1

Podcasts are the strongest predictor in the taxonomy.

15 of 18

outcomes where podcasts rank first. No other news source leads more than twice.

It leads every one of the six misinformation claims, three of the four conspiracy items, and six of the eight institutional-trust outcomes — a mean rank of 1.4 out of 7, more than two full places clear of the next regime.

Takeaway 1, continued

But it is substitution, not cynicism.

1.97×

gap between how podcasts track the establishment and how they track its challengers.

Podcast listening predicts distrust of the CDC, FDA, doctors, scientists, pharma and Fauci — and it reverses for Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Elon Musk, where listeners are 1.40× and 1.59× more trusting. Every other regime sits between 0.62 and 1.33 on that ratio.

The sign is identical in all three parties, the reversal included among Democrats.

Takeaway 2

Private messaging predicts the claims a minority holds — and nothing once a claim is common.

r = −0.855

correlation between how widely a claim is believed and private messaging's log odds ratio.

It is the strongest predictor of the CIA 9/11 claim (22% believed), election denial (31%) and wind turbines (15%) — and is negatively associated with the two most widely believed items in the battery.

Very Large Platform runs the opposite way, at r = +0.953. Because the signs differ across regimes, this cannot be the odds scale compressing near saturation — that would move every regime the same way.

Takeaway 2, continued

One behaves like transmission. The other like a milieu.

Private messaging discriminates while a claim is still confined to a minority and adds nothing once it is widespread. Very Large Platform is strongest precisely on the attitudes most people already hold — ambient exposure, not transmission.

Holding all four conspiracy items fixed does not flatten this. The gradient steepens, to r = −0.890, so conspiracy predisposition explains part of the level and none of the pattern.
