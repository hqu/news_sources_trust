The channels that cannot be counted are the ones that matter most

How political news reaches Americans, what they end up believing, and why the interventions built for platforms act on the wrong layer.
Civic Health and Institutions Project (CHIP50), June 2022 – May 2026.

In brief. Almost everything known about political misinformation comes from the handful of channels an outside analyst can count, and every major intervention — deplatforming, downranking, labels, fact-checks, the DSA's duties — attaches to exactly those channels. Across fifteen waves of a national survey we map American political news use onto seven regimes defined not by technology but by what can be observed about them, and estimate what each one predicts across 31 beliefs and questions about institutional trust. The regimes do predict belief, and not in the order platform governance assumes: on election denial, podcasts and private messaging run ahead of the large platforms that are the near-exclusive object of regulation. More than that, the regimes are not one phenomenon with different strengths. Giving each the same three candidate explanations — how common a position is overall, how much it belongs to the answerer's own side, and which side of politics it sits on — separates them into distinct kinds: podcasts track sheer rarity, partisan broadcast and the big feeds track the distance between the parties, mainstream news and word of mouth pull in opposite ideological directions, and private messaging alone tracks how out of step a position is with the user's own side, carrying no ideological direction at all. That last pattern holds on trust in the police and the military as firmly as on conspiracy claims, survives with about 45% of its strength among people who report no party whatsoever, and is therefore not a story about misinformation travelling — it is a story about who occupies a layer nobody can audit. The theoretical cost is specific: a diffusion account, which predicts that what matters is how far a claim has spread, is the wrong model for the private layer and the right one for podcasts, and the governance instruments built on the observable layer cannot reach either.

Start here: three ways a belief can be unusual

Think about where politics actually reaches you. A group chat with old friends. A podcast on the drive. Your brother-in-law at dinner. The feed. The evening news. It is tempting to sort those by how trustworthy they are — but that is not what separates them in this data. What separates them is which kind of unusual a belief has to be before a source goes with holding it.

There are three different ways a belief can be unusual, and they come apart. Almost nobody thinks wind turbines cause cancer — that belief is rare everywhere. Trusting Donald Trump is not rare at all, but it is lopsided: common on one side of politics, rare on the other. And a belief that is your own side's is by definition the other side's unusual one. Those are three separate measurements, and in this data they barely overlap.

Before any of it: nothing on this page shows a source changing anyone's mind. People choose where they get their news, and the choosing runs on the same things the beliefs do.
